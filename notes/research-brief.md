# Research brief: grounding a heritable-gene ABM of animal domestication (goats first, wolf to dog second)

Compiled 2026-10-18. This is a follow-up to the grain-domestication ABM.

**How to read the citations.** Each source carries a tag for how far I checked it in this session.
- **[V]**: I read the full text, or the relevant passages, or a full abstract during this session.
- **[A]**: I confirmed the citation and its main claim from an abstract, a repository record or a secondary summary, but did not read the full text.
- **[M]**: from prior knowledge, with no new check this session. Treat these as leads to verify before citing.

**How to read the claims.** Each claim is marked as one of the following.
- *Established*: broadly agreed and supported by multiple lines of evidence.
- *Debated*: live disagreement in the literature.
- *Weak*: little data, or the evidence is anecdotal.

## 1. The animal domestication syndrome (DS)

### 1.1 The trait suite
The traits usually listed are:
- behaviour: tameness, meaning reduced fear of and reactive aggression toward humans;
- coat: depigmentation and white spotting, and more variable coat colour;
- ears and tail: floppy ears, curly tails, shortened tails;
- skull and brain: shorter muzzles or jaws, smaller teeth, reduced brain size relative to body size;
- life history: neoteny or paedomorphosis, earlier sexual maturity, reduced reproductive seasonality or more frequent oestrus;
- physiology: a damped hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) stress response, and altered serotonin and other neurotransmitters.

Darwin described the pattern, and the term was applied to animals in the 1980s.

**No two authors list the same suite.** Lord et al. tabulated ten DS publications and found that no single trait appeared in all of them. The most common were coat colour (80%), brain size (70%) and jaw length (70%). (Lord et al. 2020, Fig. 1) [V]

### 1.2 Mechanistic hypotheses
- **Neural crest cell (NCC) hypothesis.** Wilkins, Wrangham & Fitch 2014, "The 'domestication syndrome' in mammals: a unified explanation based on neural crest cell behavior and genetics", *Genetics* 197(3):795–808. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4096361/ [A]
  - The argument: selection for tameness favours mild, polygenic reductions in NCC proliferation or migration. That reduces the NCC-derived adrenal medulla, which damps the fight-or-flight response. Because other tissues also derive from the neural crest, the same change spills over into melanocytes (white patches), facial bones (shorter snout), teeth, and ear cartilage (floppy ears).
  - Reduced forebrain size is proposed as an indirect effect.
  - The hypothesis needs a **pleiotropic** genetic architecture: many loci, each with small NCC effects.
  - Status: *Debated*. It is popular, but has not been directly tested as a unified cause.
- **Critique and reply in *Genetics*.**
  - Johnsson, Henriksen & Wright 2021, "The neural crest cell hypothesis: no unified explanation for domestication", *Genetics* 219(1):iyab097. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8633120/ [A]
  - Wilkins, Wrangham & Fitch 2021 replied in the same journal and renamed the idea the "neural crest/domestication syndrome" (NCDS) hypothesis. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8633094 [A]
- **Other hypotheses named in Lord et al. 2020** [V]:
  - paedomorphosis/neoteny: shifted developmental timing;
  - thyroid-rhythm: altered thyroid hormone release.
- **Belyaev's "destabilizing selection".** Belyaev 1979, *J. Heredity* 70:301–308 [M]. Selection on behaviour alters neuroendocrine regulation and releases hidden variation in many systems.

### 1.3 Whether DS is real across species
- **Sánchez-Villagra, Geiger & Schneider 2016**, "The taming of the neural crest: a developmental perspective on the origins of morphological covariation in domesticated mammals", *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 3:160107. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160107 [A]
  - Only canids show a large share of the predicted DS features. Other domesticates show scattered subsets.
  - Sánchez-Villagra later described Lord et al. 2020 as the "final nail in the coffin" for a universal DS (as quoted in press coverage) [A].
- **Lord, Larson, Coppinger & Karlsson 2020**, "The history of farm foxes undermines the animal domestication syndrome", *TREE* 35(2):125–136, published online Dec 2019. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2019.10.011 · OA PDF: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:78fcafc9-55d3-4675-ac3e-d9f08c33e33a [V]. Key points:
  - **The foxes were not wild.**
    - They descend from Prince Edward Island (Canada) fur farms, where foxes had been captive and purpose-bred since the 1880s.
    - Leo Frank supplied 65 pairs to found the Soviet fur industry in 1928.
    - All 24 experiment foxes tested carried Canadian mtDNA haplotypes.
    - The PEI foxes were described at the time as unusually friendly, and Belyaev also preferentially took calm founders.
    - So the rapid response looks like **selection on standing variation**.
  - **Several founder events and a small effective population size (Ne).** Large allele-frequency shifts could come from drift alone.
  - **Three criteria a DS trait should meet:**
    - Onset: it appears or rises with tameness selection.
    - Frequency: it is significantly more common in the selected population.
    - Association: tamer individuals are more likely to carry it.
    - They found no species in which the fox-reported traits met all three.
  - **Goat-relevant findings:**
    - Both wild and domestic goats have white spotting. Free-living domestic goats in India show additional colour variation.
    - There are no data comparing muzzle length in non-breed goats with wild goats, and ongoing wild admixture would complicate any comparison.
    - Floppy ears are rare outside breeds.
    - Reduced seasonality and more than one viable litter per year do appear in domestic goats. This is my reading of their Fig. 2 grid, which was hard to parse.
  - **Brain size.** Reduced relative brain size is well supported only in pigs among the species they examined. It was not observed in Belyaev's selected foxes.
  - **Their proposal.** Focus on "essential adaptations to human-modified environments", namely reduced flight distance, breeding near humans, and using anthropogenic food, rather than on DS.
- **Responses:**
  - Wright, Henriksen & Johnsson 2020, "Defining the domestication syndrome: comment on Lord et al. 2020", *TREE*. https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/defining-the-domestication-syndrome-comment-on-lord-et-al-2020/ [A]
    - Lord et al.'s criteria are too restrictive.
    - A phenotypic DS does exist, but as a **small core** of traits.
  - Lord, Larson & Karlsson 2020, "Brain size does not rescue domestication syndrome", *TREE*. https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:335568b6-b766-4aaa-b9d1-94ea9e218101 [A]. Even a purely phenotypic definition finds little support.
  - Zeder 2020, "Straw foxes: domestication syndrome evaluation comes up short", *TREE* 35(8) [M; not retrieved]. A defence of DS as a real but variable pattern.
  - Dugatkin, as quoted by Science News 2019 [A]: evidence of prior partial domestication is "slim and equivocal".
- **Definitions are moving away from DS altogether.** Lord, Larson, Allaby & Karlsson 2025, "A universally applicable definition for domestication", *PNAS* 122(22):e2413207122 [A].
  - Domestication is evolution in response to an anthropogenic niche.
  - A domestic population is one that is obligately synanthropic, that is, it cannot sustain itself outside that niche.
  - Kohl 2025 (*PNAS* 122(51):e2518220122) [V] objected that this makes "domestic" an ecological status that can change without any evolution. He argues instead for a Darwinian definition: adaptive heritable change toward higher service or resource provision.

### 1.4 The Belyaev/Trut silver fox experiment: the numbers
Primary quantitative source: Trut, Oskina & Kharlamova 2009, "Animal evolution during domestication: the domesticated fox as a model", *BioEssays* 31(3):349–360. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2763232/ [V]

- **Founders and protocol**
  - About 30 males and 100 vixens, drawn from roughly the 10% of farm foxes with the weakest fear response.
  - Started 1959.
  - Each generation, fewer than 10% of the tamest animals became parents (fewer than 20% per Wikipedia). Secondary sources give about 5% of males and 20% of females [A].
  - Selection was on tameness alone: the response to a hand or a human, tested monthly from 1 month to 6–7 months of age.
  - Over the experiment, about 10,500 parents produced about 50,000 tested offspring.
  - Inbreeding coefficients were kept at 0.02–0.07.
- **Share of "elite" (class IE) foxes by generation**

| Generation | Elite share | Count |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 1.8% | 4/213 |
| 10 | 17.9% | 66/379 |
| 20 | 35% | 503/1,438 |
| 30 | 49% | 804/1,641 |
| 2005–06 (about gen. 45+) | almost all | — |

  Trut 1999 (*American Scientist* 87:160–169) gives 70–80% elite at about generations 30–35 [A]. That conflicts somewhat with 49% at generation 30 in the 2009 paper.
- **Novel traits, domesticated vs farm-bred populations** (proportions from Trut et al. 2009, Table 2)

| Trait | Domesticated | Farm-bred |
|---|---|---|
| "Star" (white forehead blaze) | 0.124 | 0.0071 |
| Curly tail | 0.094 | 0.008 |
| Brown mottling | 0.0045 | 0.0009 |
| Floppy ears | 0.0023 | 0.00017 |
| Shortened tail | 0.0014 | 0.0002 |

  - So star and curly tail rose about 12–17-fold but still affect only 9–12% of animals. Floppy ears stay well under 1%.
  - Timing [A, Wikipedia summarising Trut 1999]: tail wagging at generation 4; star, piebald and floppy ears at about generation 10; shortened legs and tail and malocclusion after generations 15–20, in "a very small percentage".
- **HPA axis**
  - Plasma glucocorticoids were lower by generation 10.
  - By generation 20, basal cortisol was about 2-fold lower and stress cortisol about 30% lower.
  - By generation 45, basal cortisol was about 3-fold lower and stress cortisol about 5-fold lower.
  - Brain serotonin and 5-HIAA were higher, and tryptophan hydroxylase activity was higher.
- **Development**
  - Fear onset marks the close of the socialisation window. In farm foxes, exploratory behaviour drops sharply at about 45 days, when fear appears. In tame foxes this drop was absent up to 4 months.
  - This is the best-documented mechanistic link: **tameness as delayed fear onset**, which is paedomorphosis in behaviour.
- **Reproduction**
  - Sexual maturity came about 1 month earlier.
  - Some out-of-season matings (October–May), and a few vixens mated twice.
  - Lord et al. 2020: no vixen produced two viable litters in a year, and no out-of-season pups survived. They read the reproductive shift as possibly a direct effect of lower stress (fearful foxes stop breeding early) rather than a separate DS trait [V].
  - Popular accounts say litters are "one pup larger" [A]. That is not in the 2009 paper.
- **Skull.** Belyaev reported shorter, wider skulls, but Lord et al. note that cranial morphology did not distinguish the selected foxes in a formal study [V].
- **Social cognition.** Tame foxes read human gestures like dogs. Hare et al. 2005, *Curr. Biol.* 15:226–230 [M]. This is cited as evidence that dog-like social cognition is a by-product of selection against fear.
- **Genetics of fox tameness**
  - QTL on fox chromosome 12, matching a region of dog chromosome 5 implicated in early dog domestication.
  - In a 537-animal F2 cross, eight QTL with little overlap across test contexts. Tameness is polygenic and context-specific.
  - (Kukekova lab: https://publish.illinois.edu/kukekova-lab/publications/) [A]
- **Heritability of fox behaviour** (Kenttämies et al. 2006, Nordic farm silver foxes) [A]
  - "Confidence": h² = 0.22. The realised response was 0.13 points vs 0.21 predicted over 3 years.
  - Aggressiveness: h² ≈ 0.
  - Ease of capture: h² = 0.07.

### 1.5 Correlated responses: pleiotropy vs linkage vs independence
- **Plausible as pleiotropy, from one shared developmental or physiological axis** (strongest evidence first):
  1. **Fear/tameness ↔ HPA reactivity (cortisol).** Strong in foxes, rats and junglefowl. *Established* as correlated in selection lines.
  2. **Tameness ↔ timing of the socialisation window** (later fear onset). Same axis. *Established in foxes*.
  3. **Tameness ↔ reproductive timing** (earlier maturity, longer breeding season). Probably mediated by stress, so it may be **physiological rather than genetic** pleiotropy (Lord et al. 2020). *Debated*.
  4. **Tameness ↔ white spotting.** The NCC hypothesis predicts it, and the fox "star" frequency rose about 17×. But it is absent or ambiguous in goats (wild bezoar already spotted), and Neolithic goat sweeps at KIT/KITLG could reflect direct human preference. *Debated*.
  5. **Tameness ↔ craniofacial, ear and tail changes.** Rare even in foxes (under 1% for floppy ears), and there are no data for non-breed goats. *Weak*.
- **Treat as independent or separately selected:** milk yield, growth rate, body size, litter size, horn form.
  - Each has its own selection pressures, such as nutrition, culling and herder preference, and its own loci (see §2).
  - Body-size reduction in goats lags management by 500–1,000+ years (Zeder 2008; Daly et al. 2021), which argues against any tight pleiotropy with early tameness selection.
- **Reactive aggression vs fear.**
  - Different tests capture different things. In foxes, h² for aggressiveness was about 0 while confidence was 0.22. In sheep, flight time and isolation-box tests have a genetic correlation of only about −0.2 [A].
  - Model "tameness" as at least two partly correlated traits:
    - fear/flight distance toward humans;
    - reactivity or aggression in handling and isolation.
- **Simulations of the molecular DS.** Burban, Tenaillon & Le Rouzic 2022, "Gene network simulations provide testable predictions for the molecular domestication syndrome", *Genetics* 220(2):iyab214. https://academic.oup.com/genetics/article/220/2/iyab214/6440055 [A]
  - An individual-based model of gene-regulatory networks (Wagner-type) under a bottleneck plus a shift in selection.
  - It predicts a **global increase in genetic correlations** among traits after domestication.
  - So correlations can *emerge*; they need not be hard-wired from the start.

### Modelling implications (§1)
- **Core genes.** Make a polygenic **"fear/HPA reactivity"** axis the core gene set. Give it **pleiotropic outputs**:
  - flight distance from humans;
  - stress-hormone level, which feeds into reproductive success under captivity and crowding;
  - age at fear onset (the socialisation window), so juveniles exposed to humans before the window closes become habituated.
- **Separate loci, optional link.** Model pigmentation and white spotting as a few loci of large effect (KIT/KITLG-like), with an **optional, switchable pleiotropic weight** on the fear axis.
  - This lets the ABM test the NCC hypothesis (link on) against Lord et al.'s view (link off), instead of building one answer in.
- **No hard-wired craniofacial or ear traits.** If included, make them low-penetrance and weakly linked.
- **Reproductive seasonality.** Represent it as a **reaction norm**: a genetic baseline plus a stress- and nutrition-dependent shift. Feral reversion to seasonality (pigs, cats) shows a large environmental component.
- **Standing variation.** The fox data are evidence for selection on standing variation in a pre-adapted population. Initialise the wild goat population with realistic standing variance rather than relying on new mutation.

## 2. Goat domestication

### 2.1 Ancestor, place, date
- **Wild ancestor:** bezoar, *Capra aegagrus* (sometimes *C. hircus aegagrus*). *Established*.
  - All domestic goat mtDNA haplogroups occur in bezoar. Naderi et al. 2008, *PNAS* 105:17659 [A]. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/2584717/
- **Where and when**
  - Zagros and Taurus, from about 10,500–10,000 cal BP.
  - Zeder & Hesse 2000, *Science* 287:2254–2257 [A]: initial goat management in the western Iranian highlands at about 10,000 cal BP.
  - Daly et al. 2021 [V] bracket the onset of management to about 9300–8200 cal BC, between Asiab (wild-only exploitation, about 9750–9300 cal BC) and Ganj Dareh (about 8200–7600 cal BC).
- **Mosaic domestication.** Daly et al. 2018, "Ancient goat genomes reveal mosaic domestication in the Fertile Crescent", *Science* 361:85–88. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aas9411 · AAM: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/133179/ [V]
  - 83 ancient goats, 51 with genome-wide data.
  - **Multiple divergent wild sources** were domesticated in a dispersed process, giving distinct eastern (Zagros) and western (Anatolia/Levant) Neolithic populations.
  - Gerbault, Powell & Thomas 2012 (*Anthropozoologica* 47(2), ABC on mtDNA) had preferred a single ancestral population managed at about 11,500 BP [A]. That was superseded by the ancient genomes.

### 2.2 The archaeological signature of early management
- **Kill-off profiles.** Zeder & Hesse 2000 [A]; detail from Daly et al. 2021 [V].
  - Sex-specific age curves, built from sexual dimorphism in long bones, show **selective harvest of subadult males**.
  - At Ganj Dareh, 60–70% of males in all five building levels were killed before about 2.5 years. 60–70% of females survived past that age.
  - Only about 20% of goats survived beyond 4 years.
  - Females outnumbered males by 1.11–1.86 to 1.
  - This demographic shift is the earliest marker of herding. *Established*.
- **Morphology lags behaviour and demography**
  - Ganj Dareh and Tepe Abdul Hosein goats show no body-size reduction and no twisted horns; they look morphologically wild.
  - Size reduction does not appear in the Zagros until the Ceramic Neolithic, about 7000 BC (Daly et al. 2021) [V].
  - Zeder 2008 (*PNAS* 105:11597–11604) puts management at least about 1,000 years before the classic morphological markers. Horn shape and size change 500–1,000 years after the demographic shift [A]. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2575338
  - Zeder also argues that overall size reduction partly reflects culling demography: more adult females, which are smaller. That makes size a poor marker [A].
- **Horns.** Bezoar males have large scimitar horns. Domestic goats develop smaller horns, twisted in cross-section and in form, and some are polled later. In the archaeological record this appears after the demographic shift. *Established in outline*; the timing varies by region.
- **Genetic signature of management at Ganj Dareh.** Daly, Mattiangeli, … Zeder & Bradley 2021, "Herded and hunted goat genomes from the dawn of domestication in the Zagros Mountains", *PNAS* 118(25):e2100901118. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8237664/ [V]
  - **Herded and hunted goats coexisted.** A "Zagros Main" group had domestic affinity. A minority "Zagros Outlier" group had wild affinity and was probably hunted.
  - **Inbreeding.** Long runs of homozygosity in the herded goats; F_ROH>5Mb was 2.7% and 8.2% in two genomes, above the 80th percentile of all genomes tested. Y-chromosome diversity was restricted. Together these point to **few breeding males and kin mating** (tentative, small n).
  - **No sweeps yet.** No pigmentation or other domestication sweeps were found in the earliest herded goats, consistent with "management before genetic domestication".

### 2.3 Dairying
- **Lipid residues.** Evershed et al. 2008, *Nature* 455:528–531 [A]: milk fats in pottery before about 6500 BC, with the earliest and strongest signal in **NW Anatolia**, correlated with cattle abundance.
  - Where sheep and goats dominated, milk residues were less common.
  - This is evidence of processed dairy products, not necessarily of the milking of goats specifically.
- **Kill-off profiles.** Vigne & Helmer 2007, "Was milk a 'secondary product' in the Old World Neolithisation process?", *Anthropozoologica* 42(2):9–40 [A].
  - From 36 caprine and 17 cattle slaughter profiles, they argue milk exploitation was **early**, by the PPNB, and not a late "secondary products revolution".
  - Status: *Debated*. Marom & Bar-Oz 2009 (*J. Archaeol. Sci.* 36:1184–1187) [M; summarised via Triozzi & McClure 2026] showed that culling profiles cannot reliably distinguish theoretical meat, milk and wool survivorship models.
- **Genomics.** STAT1, which is linked to mammary development and milk traits in cattle, is a Neolithic eastern outlier (Daly et al. 2018) [V]. This is the only milk-related early signal and is circumstantial.

### 2.4 Traits under selection, and genomic evidence
From Daly et al. 2018 [V]: an F_ST outlier scan of Neolithic vs modern bezoar found 18 windows with high divergence and reduced diversity.

| Signal | Population | Interpretation in paper |
|---|---|---|
| **KIT**, **KITLG** | both East and West (the only shared signals) | Pigmentation and piebald. The paper suggests coat marking for **ownership in shared herds**, aesthetics, or a pleiotropic by-product of tameness. The KIT sweeps were **independent** in east and west, acting on different standing haplotypes. |
| ASIP, (MITF, TYRP1) | East | Pigmentation |
| SIRT1 | West | Stature: body-size reduction |
| EPGN | East | Calving interval: reproductive frequency |
| STAT1 | East | Mammary development: milk |
| CYP2C19 homolog | East (2nd-strongest signal) | Xenobiotic and mycotoxin metabolism: possibly a response to agricultural by-products as fodder |

Later goat studies:
- **Zheng et al. 2020**, "The origin of domestication genes in goats", *Science Advances* 6(21):eaaz5216. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7314551 [A]
  - Two strongly selected loci. **MUC6**, a gut mucin, is **introgressed from a West Caucasian tur-like species** and confers resistance to gastrointestinal pathogens. A second locus "may be related to behavior".
  - The selected alleles date to at least 7,200 and 8,100 years ago respectively.
- **Dou et al. 2023**, "A missense mutation in RRM1 contributes to animal tameness", *Science Advances* 9:eadf4068 [A]. https://pure.nwpu.edu.cn/en/publications/a-missense-mutation-in-rrm1-contributes-to-animal-tameness/
  - This identifies the second locus as **STIM1–RRM1**. Its one missense variant, **RRM1 I241V**, was present in early domestic goats about 6,500 years ago.
  - **Mice edited to carry it were tamer, more social and less anxious**, with altered glutamatergic synapses.
  - This is the best candidate for an actual tameness gene in goats. Daly et al. 2021 [V] detected the derived STIM1–RRM1 haplotype at about 19–21% frequency in Neolithic Serbia and in one Zagros genome.
  - Daly et al. 2018 did **not** report STIM1–RRM1. Attribute it to Zheng 2020 and Dou 2023.

Traits under human selection (synthesis; evidence quality varies):
- **Docility / reduced flight distance.**
  - Implied by management. There is genomic support (RRM1) for a behaviour sweep by about 6,500 BP, but not in the very earliest herds.
- **Fecundity / reproductive frequency.**
  - EPGN signal. Lord et al. 2020 count reduced seasonality and more than one viable litter per year as present in domestic goats.
  - Prolificacy in modern landraces is about 1.5 kids per kidding. Triozzi & McClure 2026 use 1.49 for goats and 1.15 for sheep.
- **Milk.** STAT1, and early dairying is plausible. Its strength is *debated* for goats specifically.
- **Meat and growth.**
  - The main early product (young-male culling).
  - Selection on growth *rate* is not documented archaeologically early. Size actually **decreased**.
- **Breeding seasonality.** Bezoar ruts roughly November–January, with 150–160 days' gestation and spring kidding [A, non-peer-reviewed profiles]. Domestic goats at low latitudes breed year-round.
- **Flocking / herding behaviour.**
  - No direct genetic or archaeological measure found. It is implied by keeping animals in mixed herds and stabling them.
  - Lord et al.'s "essential adaptations" (reduced flight distance; breeding near humans) are the defensible version of this.

### 2.5 Zeder's pathway framework
- Zeder 2012, "Pathways to animal domestication", in Gepts et al. (eds), *Biodiversity in Agriculture*, Cambridge UP, 227–259; also Zeder 2012, *J. Anthropol. Res.* 68:161–190 [M]. Discussed in Larson & Fuller 2014, *Annu. Rev. Ecol. Evol. Syst.* 45:115–136 [M].
  - **Commensal:** animals attracted to the human niche; no initial human intent (dog, cat).
  - **Prey:** hunting becomes game management, then herd management (goat, sheep, cattle, pig). **Goats are the type case.**
  - **Directed:** deliberate capture for a purpose (horse, donkey, camel).
- Critics note that pathways are inferred from present-day relationships, and that species can switch pathways (horses were prey for millennia).
- Zeder 2016 (*Evol. Ecol.* 30:325–348) treats domestication as **niche construction**. Corrals, traps and selective culling are human niche-constructing acts [A].

### Modelling implications (§2)
- **The pathway as a sequence of human behaviours, not of goat traits.**
  - The ABM should reproduce the observed order:
    1. Demographic management: sex-biased culling.
    2. Genetic differentiation: drift and inbreeding from few sires, with only a modest sweep.
    3. Pigmentation and behaviour sweeps (by about 8,000–6,500 BP).
    4. Body-size and horn change, about 500–1,500 years after management begins.
  - A good validation target: **does size reduction lag in the sim?** That depends on whether size responds to nutrition and culling demography as well as to genes.
- **Pigmentation should be a target of direct human preference** (ownership marking), separable from tameness. The independent east–west KIT sweeps from standing variation argue for a **multi-allelic standing-variation locus**, not a single new mutation.
- **Include a "tameness" locus of moderate effect** (RRM1-like) on top of the polygenic fear background. This lets a sweep be detected in the simulated genome.
- **Include an adaptive-introgression locus** (MUC6-like): a disease- or parasite-resistance allele available only from a related wild population. It shows that wild gene flow can *help* the domestic population.
- **Include a fodder-tolerance locus** (CYP2C19-like) to couple the goat ABM to the grain ABM. Goats fed crop by-products face a new selective environment.

## 3. Wolf to dog

### 3.1 Pathway and the self-domestication hypothesis
- **Commensal pathway.** *Established* as the framing; *debated* in its mechanics.
- **Scavenger / self-domestication model.** Coppinger & Coppinger 2001, *Dogs* [A].
  - Bolder, less fearful wolves exploited human refuse. Natural selection for a shorter flight distance produced proto-dogs without human intent.
- **Critique.** Serpell 2021, "Commensalism or cross-species adoption? A critical review of theories of wolf domestication", *Front. Vet. Sci.* 8:662370. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/veterinary-science/articles/10.3389/fvets.2021.662370/full [A]
  - Mobile Late Pleistocene foragers probably did not produce enough edible waste.
  - Foragers were unlikely to tolerate habituated wild wolves near camp.
  - Serpell favours **cross-species adoption**: people raising wolf pups, as seen ethnographically.
- **Lahtinen et al. 2021**, "Excess protein enabled dog domestication during severe Ice Age winters", *Sci. Rep.* 11:7. https://doaj.org/article/0087809b2603421386911fadd0bfa11f [A]
  - Human meat intake is capped by the liver's capacity to process protein, so in harsh winters lean game left a **protein surplus** that wolves, able to live on lean meat for months, could use.
  - Sharing that surplus reduced human–wolf competition.
  - Supporting point: all Pleistocene dog and proto-dog sites lie in subarctic or arctic-like environments.
  - This gives a concrete resource mechanic for the early "tolerance" phase.
- **Hunting partnership.**
  - Dogs as hunting aids raise forager returns; the evidence is ethnographic [M].
  - Probably more relevant **after** an initial tolerant phase than as the trigger.
  - The 2026 popular review (ScienceBlog, Sept 2026) still treats scavenging, adoption and cooperative hunting as competing hypotheses [A].

### 3.2 Timing
*Debated*. Ranges:
- **Earliest secure dog remains:** about 14–16 ka.
  - Bonn-Oberkassel, about 14.2 ka [M].
  - A 2026 ancient-genome study reports a confirmed dog at Pınarbaşı (Anatolia) at about 15.8 ka, with fish-rich diets suggesting people fed dogs; only press coverage seen [A].
- **Dog lineages by 11 ka.** At least five dog lineages had diversified by about 11 ka. Bergström et al. 2020, *Science* 370:557–564 [M].
- **Dual wolf ancestry.** Bergström et al. 2022, "Grey wolf genomic history reveals a dual ancestry of dogs", *Nature* 607:313–320 [A].
  - Dogs derive from at least two wolf populations: an eastern source that contributed to all dogs, and a western source that contributed up to half the ancestry of Near Eastern and African dogs.
  - Neither source population has been sampled.
- **Siberia hypothesis.** Perri et al. 2021, "Dog domestication and the dual dispersal of people and dogs into the Americas", *PNAS* 118(6):e2010083118 [A, citation only]. It proposes domestication in Siberia by about 23 ka.
- **Genetic divergence estimates** span about 15–40 ka, depending on mutation rate and the calibration genome. The Taimyr wolf points to more than 27 ka (Skoglund et al. 2015, *Curr. Biol.*) [M].

### 3.3 Reduced fear and aggression; "survival of the friendliest"
- **Self-domestication by selection against aggression.** Hare, Wobber & Wrangham 2012, "The self-domestication hypothesis: evolution of bonobo psychology is due to selection against aggression", *Anim. Behav.* 83:573–585 [A].
  - Selection against aggression yields DS-like by-products.
- **Survival of the friendliest.** Hare 2017, "Survival of the friendliest: *Homo sapiens* evolved via selection for prosociality", *Annu. Rev. Psychol.* 68:155–186 [M]. Hare & Woods 2020, *Survival of the Friendliest* (book) [A].
  - The claim: selection for friendliness drives cooperative-communicative skill and DS-like traits as by-products, in dogs, bonobos and humans.
- **Genetic candidate for hypersociability.** vonHoldt et al. 2017, *Science Advances* 3:e1700398 [A].
  - Structural variants in **GTF2I / GTF2IRD1**, in the Williams-Beuren syndrome region, are associated with hypersociability that separates dogs from wolves.
  - Small n: 18 dogs and 10 socialised wolves.
- **Diet.** The strongest dog–wolf selection signals include starch digestion (AMY2B; Axelsson et al. 2013, *Nature*) [M], as cited by Lord et al. 2020 [V].
  - This is a reminder that diet adaptation can matter as much as behaviour.
- **Reproduction**
  - Wolves are strictly monoestrous and seasonal, with a breeding season of a few days a year even in captivity.
  - Female dogs come into oestrus about every 8 months, and males are constantly receptive.
  - This is a **genetic** change. *Established* (Lord et al. 2020) [V].

### 3.4 Heritability of tameness and social behaviour in canids
- **Fox confidence:** h² = 0.22 (Kenttämies et al. 2006) [A].
- **Dogs:** behaviour is moderately heritable, but breed predicts individuals poorly.
  - MacLean et al. 2019, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 286:20190716 [A]: high **among-breed** heritability across 14 C-BARQ traits; 131 associated SNPs.
  - Morrill et al. 2022, *Science* 376:eabk0639 [A]: most behavioural traits are heritable; breed explains about **9%** of individual behavioural variation. The specific SNP-h² values were not retrieved; I recall roughly 0.1–0.5 per trait [M].

### 3.5 Existing wolf-to-dog ABM (the closest prior art for the whole project)
**Elzinga, Kulwicki, Iselin, Spence & Capaldi 2025**, "Rapid evolution of prehistoric dogs from wolves by natural and sexual selection emerges from an agent-based model", *Proc. R. Soc. B* 292:20242646. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11813586/ [V]

- **Implementation:** Python, documented with the ODD protocol, non-spatial, one tick per year, 15,000-year runs.
- **Agents:** canines with age (0–10), sex, and **one trait, "human tolerance" ∈ [0,1]**, initialised as a truncated normal (mean 0.1, s.d. 0.2).
- **Inheritance:** each pup's value is drawn **uniformly between its parents' values**, plus small Gaussian noise. With probability m = 0.01, a "mutant" is drawn uniformly on [0,1].
- **Resources:** a food pool with carrying capacity c = 500, split into human-type and wild-type food.
  - Canines compete for human food first, succeeding with probability equal to their tolerance.
  - Those that fail then compete for wild food, succeeding with probability 1 − tolerance.
  - So the trait trades off between the two food types.
- **Demography:**
  - Litter mean 6.8 (s.d. 2.2), from Parker & Luttich 1986.
  - First-year survival 0.5.
  - Age-specific non-starvation mortality, about 67% of deaths (Lovari 2007; Cubaynes 2014).
- **Optional assortative mate preference:** with pickiness p = 0.2, females consider only males within p of their own tolerance.
- **Results**
  - Without mate preference, bimodal "speciation" occurred in 0% of runs.
  - With it (default parameters), 74.2% of runs speciated; the median time to speciation was about 8,030 years.
  - Across the full sensitivity analysis, 6.7% of runs speciated, with a median of about 10,825 years.
  - Conclusion: time constraints do not falsify self-domestication, **but only if there is assortative mating**.
- **Precursors:** Valparaiso posters by Hire, Revor & Iselin 2018 (https://scholar.valpo.edu/cus/714) and Kulwicki & Capaldi 2019 (https://scholar.valpo.edu/cus/770) [A].
- The authors state that to their knowledge no prior ABM simulated dog evolution from wolves by natural selection [V].

### Modelling implications (§3)
- **Avoid blending inheritance.** Elzinga's "uniform between parents" rule erodes additive variance each generation. That is likely *why* divergence needs assortative mating to persist. Use explicit diploid loci, or the infinitesimal model with a segregation-variance term, so variance is maintained and gene flow is meaningful.
- **Dog phase:** one fear/tolerance axis (shared with the goat model) plus a **trade-off** between human-niche and wild-niche foraging, as in Elzinga.
- **Lahtinen's mechanic:** add a **winter protein-surplus** resource pulse from human camps.
- **Serpell's alternative:** a **pup-adoption** mechanic, where humans raise pups captured before the socialisation window closes. This links directly to the fear-onset gene in §1.
- **Reproductive mode as a heritable switch:** wolf monoestrous and seasonal vs dog roughly 8-month oestrus.
- **Wolf gene flow** (Bergström 2022 dual ancestry) should be continuous, not a single founding event.

### 4.2 Selection response
- **Breeder's equation:** R = h²·S = i·h²·σ_P per generation.
  - Truncation selection of the top 10% gives i ≈ 1.75; top 20% gives i ≈ 1.40; top 50% gives i ≈ 0.80.
  - With h² ≈ 0.2–0.3, culling-based selection on temperament would shift the mean by about 0.15–0.5 σ_P per generation.
- **What early herders could actually do.**
  - Early herders mostly culled **males** (sex-limited selection), so S is roughly halved.
  - Marshall et al. 2014 argue that selective breeding of **females** was largely absent early (§6).
  - A realistic early herd might apply i ≈ 0.5–1.0 on the male side only.
- **Fox benchmark** [V]. Under strong truncation (under 10% retained, both sexes), from an already semi-tame base: about 10 generations to 18% elite, 20 to 35%, 30 to about 50%. Fox generation time is about 1 year.
- **Realised vs predicted.** In the Nordic fox confidence line, the realised response was about 60% of predicted (0.13 vs 0.21 over 3 years) [A].

### 4.4 Effective population size, inbreeding and gene flow
- **Early herds were small, with few sires.** At Ganj Dareh, F_ROH was 2.7% and 8.2% in two genomes, and Y diversity was restricted (Daly et al. 2021) [V].
  - Settlement herd sizes are not directly known. Fournié et al. 2017 used ranges spanning orders of magnitude, based on 100–300 people/ha.
  - The urine-salt mass balance at Aşıklı Höyük suggests a jump from near zero to about **1 person or animal per 10 m²** of settlement around 10,000 BP. Abell et al. 2019, *Sci. Adv.* 5:eaaw0038, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6469938 [A]
- **Inbreeding depression.** Not quantified for early herds. In modern goats and sheep, inbreeding depression typically costs a few percent of trait mean per 10% F in fitness traits (litter size, survival) [M].
  - **Suggestion:** apply a load from recessive deleterious alleles explicitly, rather than a fixed penalty, so that gene flow from the wild can rescue small herds.
- **Wild–domestic gene flow was substantial.** *Established*.
  - Marshall, Dobney, Denham & Capriles 2014, *PNAS* 111:6153–6158. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4035985 [A]
    - Long-term gene flow between wild and domestic stocks was common.
    - Selective breeding of females was largely absent in early phases.
    - This undermines assumptions of severe bottlenecks and single origins.
  - Daly et al. 2021 [V]: Neolithic Serbian goats derive about **30%** of their ancestry from Epipaleolithic Turkish wild goats. In contrast, Zagros Main and East Iranian goats seem to differ by drift rather than wild introgression.
  - Zheng et al. 2020 [A]: an adaptive MUC6 introgression from a tur-like species, now nearly fixed.
  - Introgression also ran the other way: domestic goats into Alpine ibex at the MHC (Grossen et al. 2014, *PLoS Genet.*; cited in Lord et al. 2020) [V citation].

### Modelling implications (§4)
- **Default h² values:**
  - fear/flight ≈ 0.25 (range 0.1–0.35);
  - reactivity/aggression ≈ 0.15;
  - growth ≈ 0.3;
  - milk ≈ 0.2;
  - litter size ≈ 0.1;
  - age at first breeding ≈ 0.1–0.2 (no source found; low, as for reproductive traits).
  - Run sensitivity sweeps over these, since all come from modern, improved breeds.
- **Starting phenotypes and genetic correlations:**
  - Start environmental variance high and fix the phenotypic s.d. from published coefficients of variation.
  - Implement genetic correlations through shared loci with assigned effect vectors, not post hoc.
- **Generation overlap and age structure matter** for kill-off realism. Use annual ticks with seasonal birth pulses, or monthly ticks if seasonality must evolve.
- **Gene flow as a key lever:**
  - Make wild-to-herd gene flow a parameter, for example the probability that a herd female is mated by a wild buck per breeding season.
  - Start at about 0.05–0.3, so that cumulative introgression of about 30% over centuries is reachable.
  - Pair it with an explicit "restrict gene flow" human action (penning in the rut; §6).

## 5. Existing simulation and ABM work

I searched for: "agent-based model animal domestication", "simulation domestication syndrome", "computational model livestock domestication", "evolutionary simulation tameness", "herding ABM Neolithic", herd-demography and kill-off simulation, and CoMSES ("domestication", "herding", "goat").

**Bottom line.** No published ABM combines individual-based animal genomes with multiple heritable traits, human management actions, and wild gene flow, for any livestock species. The closest pieces are:
1. A single-trait, non-spatial wolf-to-dog ABM.
2. Stage-structured or IBM herd-demography models with no genetics.
3. A gene-network IBM of the molecular DS with no ecology.
4. Coalescent/ABC demographic inference.

  - Payne 1973, *Anatolian Studies* 23 [M]: meat, milk and wool profiles from Aşvan Kale mandibles.
  - Redding 1981, PhD dissertation, Univ. Michigan [M]: "energy" and "security" optimisation models, 10 age classes.

### Modelling implications (§5)
- **The novelty is defensible:** a heritable multi-trait goat genome under explicit human management *plus* wild gene flow. Frame the contribution as joining Triozzi & McClure / Fournié-style demography to Elzinga-style trait evolution, with Burban-style emergent genetic correlations as a testable output.
- **Reuse rather than reinvent the demographic layer.** Calibrate against the Triozzi & McClure stage matrix (open R code) and the Ganj Dareh sex and age profile. A minimal validation is that the sim's kill-off profile under a "cull young males" policy reproduces about 60–70% of males dead by 2.5 years and about 20% of all goats surviving past 4 years.

## 6. Human management actions that act as selection

| Action | Selective effect | Earliest evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sex- and age-biased culling** (kill subadult males, keep females) | Reduces the number of sires (Ne drops; inbreeding); weak, indirect selection on males (whichever survive to breed). Relaxes male–male competition, which may relax horn and size selection. | Ganj Dareh about 8200–7600 cal BC [V]; Aşıklı Höyük about 8200 cal BC or earlier (sheep-focused; Stiner et al. 2014, *PNAS* 111:8404–8409) [A] | *Established, early* |
| **Culling by temperament** (kill flighty or aggressive animals, keep calm ones) | Direct selection on fear/reactivity | Not directly visible archaeologically. Inferred from the RRM1 sweep (by about 6,500 BP) [A] and from ethnography | *Inferred*; plausible early but undocumented |
| **Confinement / penning / stabling** | Isolates the herd (cuts wild gene flow); crowding selects for stress tolerance; enables controlled mating | Primary dung layers inside Aşıklı Höyük by about 8200 cal BC; Stiner et al. call stabling "one of the early mechanisms of caprine population isolation" [A]. Urine-salt densities about 10,400–9,700 BP (Abell et al. 2019) [A] | *Established, early* (Anatolia) |
| **Translocation beyond the native range** | Founder effect; no local wild gene flow; new environment | Goats taken to Cyprus in the early PPNB, about 10,600 cal BP (Vigne et al. 2012, *PNAS* 109:8445) [M] | *Established, early* |
| **Fodder provisioning** (crop by-products) | Diet and toxin adaptation (CYP2C19 signal); better nutrition gives earlier maturity and larger litters (plastic, not genetic) | Genomic hint in the Neolithic East (Daly 2018) [V]; isotopic and dung evidence site-specific [M] | *Plausible early; patchy evidence* |
| **Protection from predators** | Relaxes selection for vigilance and flight; favours lower reactivity | Not documented directly for the earliest herds. Dogs were present in Neolithic villages; specialised guardian dogs are much later [M] | *Weak / inferred* |
| **Milking** | Selection on milk yield and let-down tolerance (handling docility) | Lipid residues before 6500 BC (NW Anatolia, cattle-linked) [A]; argued from PPNB kill-off profiles (Vigne & Helmer 2007) [A]; STAT1 [V] | *Debated for the earliest goats; established by the 7th millennium BC* |
| **Choice of sires** (keeping a few favoured males) | Strong selection through the male line; the main route to rapid change | The Ganj Dareh profile implies a few males kept [V]. Deliberate sire choice for traits is not documented early. Marshall et al. 2014: female selection largely absent early [A] | *Implied (few sires), intent unknown* |
| **Restricting gene flow with wild animals** | Allows divergence and the spread of sweeps | Contradicted for the early phases: gene flow was long-term and substantial (Marshall 2014; Daly 2021, about 30% wild ancestry in Neolithic Serbia) [A/V] | *Later and partial* |
| **Selection for coat colour / marking** | Pigmentation sweeps (KIT/KITLG), possibly for ownership in shared herds | By about 8,000 BP (Daly 2018) [V] | *Established genetically; motive debated* |
| **Castration** | Removes males from the breeding pool | Later (Bronze Age-era evidence, in my recollection) [M] | *Later* |

### Modelling implications (§6)
- **Human agents as a parameterised policy bundle.** Each herd household chooses:
  - culling (sex, age, temperament threshold);
  - sire retention (how many, by what criterion);
  - penning intensity (cuts the wild-mating probability; raises density and stress);
  - fodder level (boosts growth and fecundity plastically; imposes a fodder-tolerance selection);
  - milking (offtake trade-off with kid survival, as in the meat-vs-milk profiles);
  - predator protection (lowers wild-predator mortality).
- **Order the defaults chronologically.** Early-phase humans should have culling, penning and translocation on, with temperament culling weak or off, sire choice implicit (few males survive) rather than trait-based, and gene-flow restriction low.
  - This tests whether **demographic management alone** generates domestication sweeps, which is the central empirical puzzle that Daly 2021 sets up: morphologically wild but genetically distinct herds.
- **Couple to the grain ABM** through fodder (by-products), labour, and competition for land between herding and cultivation.

### Main uncertainties to carry into the model
1. Whether DS-type traits are genetically linked to tameness (NCC pleiotropy) or arise independently. Make this a switch.
2. Goat temperament h² has never been estimated. Use sheep and cattle values with sensitivity analysis.
3. Early herd size and Ne. Use the ROH evidence (small, few sires) and sweep across a range.
4. The wild gene-flow rate. Calibrate so that about 30% cumulative wild ancestry is reachable over centuries, and also test near-zero.
5. Wild bezoar life-history values (age at first breeding, twinning rate, lifespan, generation time) rest on weak sources. The IUCN assessment or specialist bezoar field studies are the next places to look.
